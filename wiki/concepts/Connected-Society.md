## Definition

From [[plurality-book]] quoting [[Danielle-Allen]] ("Towards a Connected Society", 2017): a Connected Society is one **"where bridging ties, across lines of difference, are formed at a high rate"**. This vision supplies the *normative* component of the three-part definition of [[Plurality]] — diversity as fuel for social progress that must be harnessed, not merely tolerated.

## Intersecting affiliations, not isolated atoms

The book frames Connected Society as the social-philosophical mirror of complexity-science views of the natural world. Where "monist atomism" (the shared root of Libertarianism and Technocracy) treats society as either a heap of individuals or a unified whole, the Plural / Connected view sees:

- people as bundles of overlapping group memberships (religion, profession, ethnolinguistic, ideology, gender, ability, generation, etc.);
- groups as themselves intersecting and dynamic, constantly forming and reconfiguring;
- the **bidirectional equilibrium** between diversity of people and the social groups they create as the essence of social science (the book cites Mark Granovetter, *Economic Action and Social Structure*).

## Many dimensions of diversity

[[plurality-book]] lists an explicitly long, non-exhaustive set of dimensions along which Connected Society sees difference: religion, jurisdiction, geographic type, profession, organizations, ethno-linguistics, race/caste/tribe, ideology, education, epistemology/field, gender and sexuality, abilities, generation, species. Identity emerges from **combinations and intersections** of these — analogous to how four DNA base pairs yield biological diversity.

## Bridging is productive, not just defensive

A key claim from [[plurality-book]] is that bridging across difference plays a **productive** role, not just a defensive one against conflict. Bridges between disciplines create new fields (behavioral economics, systems biology, data science); cultural bridging produces "bricolage" and fusion. This is why the book pairs Connected Society with the goal of **regenerating diversity** — bridges that consume diversity without replenishing it threaten the resource on which future cooperation depends.

## Open Questions

- How is the *rate* of bridging-tie formation measured in practice? The book uses social-network notions but does not propose a single metric.
- Connected Society as articulated is highly cosmopolitan; how does it relate to communities whose self-definition includes resistance to bridging (religious enclaves, cultural preservation movements)?
