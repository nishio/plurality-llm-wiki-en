## Definition

From [[plurality-book]] chapter **5-7 Social Markets**, Social Markets are market mechanisms that **internalize what neoclassical economics dismisses as externalities** — supermodularity, group-level effects, increasing returns, heterogeneity of goods, diversity of information.

Whereas conventional capitalism reduces interaction to bilateral transactions between symmetric atomistic agents, Social Markets are designed to:

- harness **supermodularity** ("the whole is greater than the sum of the parts") as an economic engine, not a footnote;
- explicitly weight contributions across **social distance**;
- avoid the monopolizing, socially-irresponsible failure modes of unmodified markets while preserving their ability to coordinate strangers across diversity.

## Mechanisms

- **[[Quadratic-Funding]]** — the chapter's anchor example, funding public goods in proportion to the diversity of small donors.
- **[[Data-Coalitions]]** — collective bargaining structures for shared data interests.
- **[[Plural-Management-Protocol]]** — the credit / governance system used to govern the [[plurality-book]] itself.
- **Reform of corporate governance**, labor markets and financial markets along Plural lines (sketched in the chapter and revisited in Section 6 Impact / Workplace).

## Theoretical grounding

The chapter grounds the construction in:

- **Supermodularity** as a generalization of comparative advantage (Aristotle → David Ricardo → modern Plural economics; the book cites Siddarth, Prewitt, Weyl 2024, "Beyond Public and Private").
- **Albert Hirschman**'s *The Passions and the Interests* on markets as civilizing.
- A Plural critique of the "fundamental welfare theorems" of neoclassical economics: their conditions are extremely circumscribed; reality is dominated by the cases they exclude.

## Capitalism today

The book takes care to acknowledge the historical achievements of global capitalism — markets are still the most consistent pattern of "rule of law" across the planet — while documenting its failures: monopolization, environmental damage, surveillance. Social Markets are presented as a **reform** that retains capitalism's reach across diversity but replaces its atomist substrate.
