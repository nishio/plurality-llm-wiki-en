## Definition

From [[plurality-book]] chapter **7-0 Policy**, "Policy" in the Plural sense names a deliberate alternative to the three dominant policy models that legal scholar [[Anu-Bradford]] in *Digital Empires* identifies:

- the **US** model — neoliberal, private-sector-driven free market;
- the **PRC** model — state-driven, national-security and party-aligned tech development;
- the **EU** model — regulatory powerhouse importing technology but defending fundamental rights (GDPR, AI Act, DSA, DMA).

The Plural model — exemplified by Taiwan — is presented as a fourth path: **active public investment** to empower **privately complemented but civil-society-led** technology development whose goal is to **proactively build a digital stack that embodies in protocols the principles of human rights and democracy**.

## Lessons from ARPA / IPTO

The chapter's historical anchor is [[JCR-Licklider]]'s leadership of the Information Processing Techniques Office (IPTO) at [[ARPA]] in the 1960s, which:

- seed-funded a network of "coopetitive" research labs (MIT, Stanford, UC Berkeley, Carnegie, UCLA);
- pursued a *vision of computing* (human–computer symbiosis, attack-resilient networking) rather than industrial-policy "national champions";
- accepted that the **failure to sustain** this investment by the 1970s led to the monopolization that today dominates digital life.

Plurality, per the book, builds closely on Lick's unfinished vision. See [[The-Lost-Dao]].

## Updates for today

The book argues the ARPA model needs adaptation because today's world is multi-polar, the open-source community is a primary developer, and military-industrial leadership is no longer the right locus. Updates include:

- **Digital ministries** as the natural national homes for Plural policy (Taiwan 2022, Ukraine, Japan's Digital Agency 2021, the EU's Digital portfolio).
- **Transnational networks** of digital ministries, civil society, and standards bodies to develop and steward protocols.
- Direct funding for **open-source / Third Sector** developers, which currently receive a small fraction of what venture capital and academic research receive (the book shows a chart comparing cumulative open-source funding to a single year of VC).
- New legal frameworks for **[[Data-Coalitions]]**, DAOs and other transnational democratic non-profits.

The proposed scale: **1% of GDP** spent on [[Digital-Public-Infrastructure]].

## Taiwan's Presidential Hackathon

A live example: an annual event in Taiwan since 2018, using [[Quadratic-Voting]] for public selection of top teams, where winners earn a presidential commitment to scale their work into national infrastructure. The book treats this as one prototype of Plural industrial policy.

## Open Questions

- The book mostly addresses democratic states. How does Plural policy interact with policymakers in authoritarian or weak-state contexts?
- A 1%-of-GDP commitment is roughly equivalent to defense spending in many states — building the political coalition for that is unaddressed in the book.
